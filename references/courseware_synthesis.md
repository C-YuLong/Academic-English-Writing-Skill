## Main Additions to the Skill

### 1. Crisp sentence writing

The courseware repeatedly returns to sentence architecture: subject, predicate verb, objects/complements, adverbials, independent clauses, and dependent clauses. The writing skill should therefore treat sentence clarity as a structural issue, not only a grammar issue.

A revised sentence should make the main subject and main verb easy to find.

### 2. Paragraphs must guide the reader

Paragraphs function as reader pauses and sense-making units. A paragraph should not be a random block of text. It should normally begin with a topic sentence and then develop one controlling idea through support and explanation.

The skill should not merely polish words inside a weak paragraph. It should check whether the paragraph has a topic sentence and whether the remaining sentences support that topic sentence.

### 3. Topic sentence + support + analysis

A good academic paragraph usually follows this pattern:
- topic sentence;
- supporting evidence;
- explanation or evaluation;
- link to the larger argument.

This overlaps with PEEL: Point, Evidence, Explanation/Evaluation, Link.

For English paper writing, the first sentence of every natural paragraph should normally serve as the topic sentence.

### 4. Information flow matters

The courseware emphasizes that sentence subjects often help create flow by picking up given information from the previous sentence. The skill should therefore check whether a sentence begins with information that the reader already knows before moving to new information.

This principle is especially useful when translating from Chinese, where English readers may expect more explicit sentence-to-sentence continuity.

### 5. Active and passive voice are choices, not slogans

The passive voice can be appropriate in academic writing, especially when the procedure matters more than the actor or when passive voice helps place given information in subject position. However, active voice is often shorter and clearer when the agent matters.

The skill should choose voice according to rhetorical function:
- active for contribution, interpretation, and agent-focused statements;
- passive for methods, procedures, and given-information subjects.

### 6. Tense choices carry meaning

The skill should not apply one tense mechanically. Use:
- present tense for general knowledge, theories, interpretations, paper purpose, and figures/tables;
- past tense for completed experiments, methods, and specific results;
- present perfect for prior research connected to the current study;
- passive voice when the method/procedure is foregrounded.

In introductions, combine these choices with CARS:
- present for territory;
- present perfect for prior work and gaps;
- present for the current paper's purpose and contribution.

### 7. Qualification must match evidence

Claims should be neither exaggerated nor over-hedged. Use claim verbs carefully:
- `show` or `demonstrate` for strong direct evidence;
- `indicate` or `suggest` for weaker or indirect evidence;
- `may`, `might`, `could`, and `tend to` for limited evidence.

The courseware warns that excessive hedging can make clear results sound weak. State clear findings clearly, and place limitations separately.

### 8. Articles and definiteness are meaning choices

Article choice is tied to information status:
- `a/an` often introduces new singular countable information;
- `the` identifies known, specific, or previously introduced information;
- no article is often used for general plural nouns and uncountable nouns.

The skill should check countability and definiteness in every polished paragraph, especially for second-language writers.

### 9. Academic register should be formal but readable

The courseware distinguishes formal register from informal language and from unnecessarily pompous language. Academic writing should be clear, straightforward, objective, evidence-based, and respectful. It should avoid colloquialisms, slang, biased terms, clichés, needless nominalization, and pretentious vocabulary.

### 10. Discipline-specific conventions matter

The writing guide and sample articles show that academic writing varies by discipline. Scientific/medical papers often use IMRaD and structured abstracts. Humanities papers may use a contextual introduction, thematic sections, and more interpretive argumentation. The skill should therefore read recent papers from the user's venue and field before substantial drafting.

### 11. Source use is part of writing

The guide emphasizes reading and using sources, relevance and reliability, reporting verbs, direct quotation limits, and academic integrity. The skill should help integrate sources through framing, paraphrase, and explanation, rather than dropping citations into a paragraph without analysis.

### 12. Revision should move from global to local

The guide distinguishes global revision from local proofreading. The skill should first check argument, structure, paragraph logic, topic sentences, and evidence. Only then should it polish grammar, articles, punctuation, tense, and word choice.
